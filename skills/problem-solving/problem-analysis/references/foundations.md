# Foundations and change rationale

This skill is a problem-driven heuristic, not a theory of everything. The sources below explain why its moves exist. They are used as defeasible guides, not as laws, and they disagree with one another in places.

## Transfer table

| Source | Claim used | Where it appears | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schön (1983) | Problems are not given; problem setting names what we attend to and frames the context of attention. | Reconstruct the problem; surface frames; reframing as progress. | A practitioner epistemology, not a procedure for deciding which frame is true. |
| Mills (1959) | Personal troubles and public issues are different levels of analysis, with different remedies. | The level question in framing. | A sociological imagination, not a general typology of problems. |
| Bacchi (2009) | Every policy or proposal contains an implicit problem representation; ask what is problematized, what is left out, and what effects follow. | Who defines the problem, what is excluded, what would count as a solution. | Critical policy analysis; it does not by itself choose an action. |
| Hisschemöller & Hoppe (1995); Hoppe (2018) | Problem structure depends on certainty of knowledge and agreement on values; structured, semi-structured, and unstructured problems need different treatment. Good problem definitions fit a feasible solution, an actor's intervention perspective, and a real opportunity for improvement. | The two-axis classification; the value-conflict boundary; the "right problem" test; safe-to-fail probes. | Developed for policy problems. The typology is analytical, and "structured" is a temporary achievement, not an objective property. |
| Rittel & Webber (1973) | Wicked problems have no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, and no true-or-false solution; each attempt changes the problem. | The wicked row; the ban on promising definitive solutions; review triggers. | The concept is contested and often overused; not every hard problem is wicked. |
| Simon (1973) | Ill-structured problems require decomposition, search, and satisficing rather than a single optimizing algorithm. | Smallest method set; stopping conditions. | A cognitive and design framing; it underplays power and value conflict. |
| Abbott (2004) | Methods are explanatory programs, not a fixed menu; problem, data, method, and theory co-evolve; heuristics should not be reified. | Problem-driven stance; method table caveat; anti-cargo-cult rule. | Abbott argues against a deterministic method-question map. This skill uses fit as a defeasible reason, not a formula. |
| Alvesson & Sandberg (2011) | Gap-spotting reproduces assumptions; problematization challenges the assumptions behind the question and can produce better questions. | Surface the problem representation; examine theory assumptions. | Aimed at theory development; do not problematize every practical decision. |
| Prasad (2021) | Problem-solving moves from description to causes, to what works, to why solutions are or are not implemented. Critique without construction, description of victims, and "going meta" can substitute for action. Inaction is also a choice. | Options including no action; the boundary against description and critique; completion. | A sociological research agenda, not a mandatory sequence for every problem. |
| Davis & Marquis (2005) | Organization theory shifted from paradigm-driven to problem-driven work; problems can mobilize multiple theories and mechanisms. | Theory as a tool; one problem may need several lenses. | A claim about organization studies, not a universal law. |
| Ragin (1987) | Case-oriented and variable-oriented methods answer different kinds of causal questions; method should fit the structure of the cases and the causal complexity. | Comparison as a method; fit over prestige. | Comparative social science; not a complete decision procedure. |

## Reconciliation: structure selects, but does not dictate

Abbott warns that there is no clean list of methods suited to particular questions. Hoppe and Rittel show that problem structure changes what kind of answer is possible. These claims are compatible if "fit" means a defeasible reason rather than a rule:

- The problem structure constrains the **kind of answer** and the **evidence** that could support it.
- It does not select a unique method. Several methods can answer the same question, and mixed methods are often appropriate.
- The method must still earn its place by producing evidence that could change the recommendation.

## What changed from version 0.2, and why

| Change | Reason |
|---|---|
| Kept the trigger, non-trigger routing, and "do not manufacture uncertainty" rule. | They prevent the skill from swallowing factual questions, assessments, bug diagnosis, and scoped work. |
| Kept "moves, not stages" and reframing. | Schön and Abbott show that problem, evidence, method, and theory co-evolve; a rigid sequence would misdescribe the work. |
| Added the context sweep and a working problem statement. | The old version said to reuse context but did not require reconstructing the problem from files, data, project artifacts, and prior attempts. This is the "know my problem" step. |
| Added the two-axis classification. | The old version treated every open problem as an evidence gap. Hoppe shows that contested values are a different structure and need a different response. |
| Added problem representation and alternative frames. | The old version assumed the question was given. Schön and Bacchi show that the question is constructed and that the frame selects evidence and remedies. |
| Added method and theory selection. | The old version never mentioned theory and implied a single evidence-first route. Abbott and Prasad show that methods and theories are resources selected for the job, not a fixed pipeline. |
| Added a single recommendation with alternatives, assumptions, reversal conditions, first step, and checkpoint. | The old version said "choose the next step" but did not require a recommendation or make its conditions explicit. |
| Added completion conditions. | The old stop rule ("evidence sufficient") does not cover value conflicts, wicked problems, or missing authority. The new list makes a conditional or blocked answer a valid completion. |
| Kept decision-relevant evidence, least-cost checks, disconfirming evidence, conditional recommendations, and scope limits. | These remain correct and are supported by the evidence-first stance of the old version and by Prasad's warning against analysis as avoidance. |

## What this skill does not claim

- It is not a validated psychological or decision-science model.
- It does not replace domain expertise, legal advice, medical advice, or the judgment of the people who bear the consequences.
- The classification is a heuristic. Reasonable people can classify the same problem differently, and the classification should be revised when evidence arrives.
- The sources disagree about how tightly methods should be matched to problems. The skill keeps the weaker, safer claim: structure constrains the answer and the evidence, but does not dictate a unique method.
